How a Home Assistant dashboard card should show and control a June oven running the Project July local server: what the oven can tell us, what the original app and oven screen did, what the best research says, five design directions, and the one we recommend building.

Recommendation

Build Glass: the oven’s own screen, as a calm card that opens into a cooking panel

  1. On the dashboard: June’s ring with the big number inside it, the uncropped camera window beside it, one status sentence, a current-versus-target bar while preheating, and an always-visible Stop. When idle: clock, camera and the oven’s mode tiles, which open a review step and never heat on one tap.
  2. Appearance: Match Home Assistant (default, follows each viewer’s light or dark theme), Light, or Dark (the oven’s glass).
  3. Behind a tap: Now · Cook · Camera · History, with Settings behind a labelled gear in the panel header. Card look lives in the card editor; the oven address stays in the integration’s Configure screen.
  4. Several ovens: one card per oven by default; an optional switcher or list keeps every oven’s live state visible. See Several ovens.
  5. Safety asymmetry: stopping is one tap, never confirmed. Starting heat is two deliberate steps that restate what will happen, and remote-start permission stays on the oven.

01 · The brief

A card for a heating appliance, not a gadget

June’s cloud was switched off on 22 September 2026. Project July keeps the oven working by running a small server on the oven itself, and our Home Assistant integration talks to it. The card is where most owners will now meet their oven away from its door, so it inherits three responsibilities the June app used to carry: telling people what the oven is doing, letting them adjust or stop a cook, and making sure nothing heats up that shouldn’t.

Who it’s for

Many June owners are older and were promised an oven that “just works”. NN/g found adults 65+ are 43% slower on interfaces and blame themselves for 90% of failures. Many HA households also mount a tablet in the kitchen and read it from 2–3 m away.

Job to be done

Christensen’s framing: the progress someone is trying to make in a circumstance. Here: keep cooking while I do other things, and know without looking when to act. Reviewers praised June’s notifications far more than its app or camera, which supports that framing.

Constraints

It must feel native to Home Assistant, use July’s design system rather than June’s brand, work from phone to wall tablet, and show only data the local server really provides. The interior camera is a required component of every design. Remote start is treated as the riskiest action on the page.

Why safety leads. Cooking causes 44–49% of US home fires, and unattended cooking is involved in about half of cooking-fire deaths (NFPA, 2023). In August 2019 several June ovens preheated overnight, which June attributed to accidental taps and voice commands, and it responded by adding a setting to disable remote preheat. Every major oven maker now gates remote start behind an action at the oven (GE Remote Enable, Samsung Smart Control, Miele MobileStart), and Traeger doesn’t allow remote ignition at all.

03 · Design language

Borrow June’s behaviour, keep July’s identity

The on-oven screen and the June app share one idea worth keeping: the oven shows you its heat. Home-screen tiles are glowing glass squares whose icon is the pattern of heating elements each mode uses. Bake is a bottom bar, broil a top bar, roast both, and air fry a fan. The app’s “isometric oven” lit each element as it switched on, and the cooking screen was one ring, one big thin number, three lines of context and one orange button. That is a disciplined, glanceable language, and every screen in the app followed it.

04 · What research says

Twelve findings that shaped every option

Eight parallel research strands covered Home Assistant’s own design direction, the June product and its reviews, award-winning appliance and smart-home interfaces, safety standards, foundational usability research, cooking-probe and prediction apps, designing for older adults and wall tablets, and camera and notification surfaces. These are the findings that changed the design.

1 · Status first, always visible

Nielsen’s first heuristic, and Endsley’s three levels of situation awareness: perception (“312°F”), comprehension (“preheating, not ready”), projection (“ready in about 4 min”). A card that shows only a number does only the first.

Nielsen, NN/g (1994, rev. 2024); Endsley, Human Factors (1995)

2 · Bars beat gauges for “how close am I?”

Few built the bullet graph “to replace the meters and gauges”. NN/g’s dashboard work says gauges take space and read worse than linear marks. A smartwatch study found bars and donuts fastest only for a single proportion. So: rings for a single timer, bars for current versus target.

Few, Perceptual Edge (2013); Laubheimer, NN/g (2017); Blascheck et al., IEEE TVCG (2019)

3 · Honest progress, honest estimates

Accelerating progress is preferred and slowdowns near the end feel worst (Harrison, CMU), yet oven heating naturally slows near its target. Map preheat progress so the last 10% doesn’t crawl, but show “Ready” only when the sensor reaches target. Estimates get an explicit “Estimating…” state and rounded numbers, like MEATER and Combustion.

Harrison et al., UIST 2007; Kay et al., CHI 2016; Padilla, Kay & Hullman (2020); MEATER, Combustion Inc. docs

4 · Stopping is easy, starting is deliberate

UL 1026 always allows remote cancel and expires remote programming after 5 minutes. GE, Samsung and Miele require arming at the oven, and opening the door disarms. Matter’s on/off cluster has an “Off only” feature. Nielsen: confirm only serious actions, or people learn to click through.

Intertek SUN, UL 1026 (2015); GE, Samsung, Miele support; CSA Matter 1.3; Nielsen, NN/g (2018)

5 · Glanceable from across the kitchen

Nest’s Farsight shows one large item from about 3 m and more detail when you approach. Alexa’s 10-ft type scale sets display text at 148–192 dp. At 2 m a numeral needs about 13–18 mm of cap height, roughly 110–150 CSS px on a 10-inch tablet. A simple rendering with few states beats a rich one (Matthews et al.).

Google Nest (2024); Amazon APL style guide; ADA 703.5.5; Matthews et al., DIS 2006

6 · Built for older hands and eyes

Older adults tapped accurately at 14 mm or more, and performance plateaued around 20 mm. Dragging is the hardest gesture, slow taps become “holds”, and people preferred explicit buttons to sliders. WCAG 2.2 requires a non-drag alternative. So: steppers and preset chips, no long-press, activate on release.

Leitão & Silva (2012); Yu et al., IJERPH (2022); Kobayashi et al., INTERACT 2011; Motti et al. (2013); WCAG 2.5.7

7 · Colour means something, or nothing

High-performance industrial displays stay neutral and reserve colour for abnormal or active states (ISA-101). IBM Carbon requires at least three of colour, shape, symbol and text for status. Heat ramps must rise in lightness to stay colour-blind safe, and red stays reserved for faults.

ISA-101; IBM Carbon; Crameri et al., Nature Communications (2020); Nuñez et al., PLOS ONE (2018)

8 · Alerts are the product

Reviewers called June’s notifications “massively helpful” and forgot the camera within a week. 85–99% of hospital alarms need no action, which teaches people to ignore them. Send few alerts, each tied to an action, one per event, replaced in place, with the countdown in a Live Activity or Live Update.

TechCrunch (2016); InsideHook (2022); Joint Commission SEA 50 (2013); Apple HIG Live Activities

9 · The camera is always there, and always honest

The camera is a required part of every design: it’s June’s signature and the fastest reassurance that food isn’t burning. The research governs how it behaves. Google, Arlo and Ring all time-box live video, and Nest Hub Max separates “camera on” from “someone is watching”. HA’s own cards refresh stills every 10 s and rejected faster polling. So the card shows a still with its frame age, goes live only when expanded, and never lets the picture replace the status sentence.

Google Nest help; Arlo KB; HA picture-entity docs and community PR; Advanced Camera Card docs

10 · Feel native to Home Assistant

HA’s card principles: “a clear primary action”, clickable things look clickable, features beat icon buttons, don’t clutter. Use the 56 px row grid, getGridOptions, theme variables with fallbacks, a visual editor, and getEntitySuggestion so the card appears in the 2026.6 card picker.

Mak, HA blog “Dashboard chapter 2” (2024); HA developer docs; HA 2026.6 release notes

11 · Model it as a statechart

Harel statecharts with concurrent regions (heat × timer × probe × connection) rule out impossible screens like “Ready” while off. The oven already speaks in steps (preheat → preheat_and_hold → cook_bound → complete_and_off), which map almost one-to-one onto Matter’s oven phases.

Harel (1987); XState/Stately; CSA Matter Oven Cavity Operational State

12 · Calm by default

Weiser and Brown: information lives in the periphery and comes to the centre only when needed. Amber Case: technology can communicate without speaking. No motion at rest, a subtle warmth only while heating, and nothing that flashes. Always-on wall panels need a dim, pixel-shifted idle state.

Weiser & Brown, Xerox PARC (1995); Case, Calm Technology; Apple StandBy; WCAG 2.2.2 and 2.3

05 · Five directions

Five ways to form the card

Each direction is a complete answer to the same brief, drawn in July’s tokens and filled with values from a real bake captured on 22 September (convection bake, 400°F, 25-minute timer, probe target 145°F). Use the switcher to put every option in the same state. The comparison matters most in Preheating, Done and Offline, where cards usually fail.

Every mockup is drawn the way Home Assistant really lays out a card. In a Sections view a section is 320–500 px wide and split into 12 columns; rows are 56 px with 8 px gaps. A card declares its size in grid cells with getGridOptions, and its height is fixed at rows × 56 + (rows − 1) × 8 px. The content has to fit that box; it never grows the card. The dashed outline and faint cells show the section grid. Switch to the phone width to see the same card squeezed into a 358 px section: the height stays the same and only the width changes.

Option A

Tile

Looks like it shipped with Home Assistant.

An HA tile card with oven-specific feature rows: a 4:3 camera picture (HA’s picture-card convention), a mode row, a target stepper and one action. Status reads as one line of text next to a state-coloured icon, exactly like HA’s built-in climate tile. Tapping the picture opens live view in the standard more-info dialog.

Strengths

  • Zero learning curve for HA users (Jakob’s law)
  • Can ship as customCardFeatures on the stock tile
  • Cheapest to build and maintain

Weaknesses

  • Temperature is small; fails the 2 m glance test
  • “How close to ready?” is text only
  • Nothing of June’s feel survives
  • HA feature rows are 42 px, below our 48 px rule
  • Only 175 px wide in a phone section

Option B

Glass

The oven’s own screen, brought to the dashboard.

A faithful reinterpretation of the on-oven and app cooking screens: one ring, one big number, a few lines of context, one button. The camera is the oven’s window: the full, uncropped 4:3 frame above the ring, with its own Live badge, that expands on tap. The big number stays inside the ring, exactly where June put it. When idle it becomes the oven’s home screen: a clock and a camera window above pages of glowing mode tiles whose icons are their heating elements, swiped left and right with page dots, ending with a Settings tile. It uses July’s tokens and glyphs, not June’s carrot or DIN, and follows the Glass appearance switch at the top of the page.

Strengths

  • Strongest emotional continuity for June owners
  • Camera reads as the oven’s window, number stays in the ring
  • Beautiful on a dark wall panel

Weaknesses

  • Ring reads worse than a bar for temperature
  • First drawn always dark; the appearance options (Match HA, Light, Dark) came with the revision
  • Idle mode tiles invite one-tap remote starts
  • Ring shrinks to 150 px in a phone section

Option C

Status line

Say what’s happening, show how close, offer one action.

Built from the research: a plain-language status sentence (Endsley’s comprehension and projection), a very large tabular number with the camera still beside it, and Few’s bullet graph with a dot for the current temperature, a tick for the target and a dashed projection. The card is neutral at rest, and colour appears only for active or abnormal states (ISA-101). One full-width action: Stop while heating, Start cooking… when off.

Strengths

  • Best glanceability and accessibility
  • Status survives greyscale and colour-blindness
  • Natural glance, standard and compact densities

Weaknesses

  • Plain; less delight than Glass
  • Camera still is small at phone width (tap to enlarge)
  • Bullet graphs are unfamiliar to some people (a label fixes this)

Option D

Window

Look through the door.

The camera is the card. Temperature, status and actions sit over a gradient at the bottom of the frame, with a frame-age badge top left. When the camera is off it shows an honest placeholder and an option to switch it on for two minutes.

Strengths

  • The largest, clearest camera of the five
  • Instant reassurance that food isn’t burning

Weaknesses

  • Status has to share space with the picture
  • Text over video is hard to keep legible (1.4.3)
  • Bandwidth and privacy cost on always-on panels
  • Empty when idle, which is most of the day

Option E

Timeline

Where are we in this cook?

The cook as a journey: Preheat → Cook → Done, like a delivery tracker driven only by real oven steps. The current stage fills, the headline is the time that matters now next to a camera still, and the probe gets its own small bar. The same layout, minus the camera, maps directly onto an iOS Live Activity or Android Live Update; Live Activities can’t show images, so the picture stays on the card and in notifications.

Strengths

  • Makes the preheat → cook model obvious
  • Goal-gradient: people see the end coming
  • One design across card, lock screen and watch (the camera is card-only)

Weaknesses

  • Has little to say when the oven is off
  • Stage widths imply durations we don’t know
  • Wide; awkward in a 6-column section

05b · The live oven view

How each candidate shows the camera, and why

The camera is required in every design. Its placement was audited against the research after the first version, because some placements were the study team’s improvisation rather than evidence. The rules below come from the research; the table marks what each candidate does and what changed.

What the research says

  • Keep the whole frame. camerad sends 640 × 480 (4:3). The oven is top-down fisheye and reviewers found blind spots at the tray edges (Brava), so never crop to a circle or strip. HA’s own picture cards expose aspect_ratio and fit_mode for the same reason.
  • Status stays primary. The camera is a secondary, tap-to-open still (NN/g cards; older-adult and wall-tablet research). June kept live video on its own tab next to “Cooking”, and Anova does the same.
  • Label every frame’s age. “Live”, then “Updated 14 s ago” (Google Home’s timestamped previews; Apple’s stale-thumbnail backlash; NN/g visibility of status).
  • Live only when expanded, for 5 minutes. Google, Arlo and Ring time-box live video; HA refreshes card stills every 10 s.
  • Two indicators. “Camera on” is separate from “someone is watching” (Nest Hub Max).
  • Text over video needs a scrim to meet WCAG 1.4.3, as HA’s picture-glance bar does.

Where the evidence runs out

No source sets a minimum thumbnail size or says whether the camera belongs above, beside or below the number on a card. Those choices are the study team’s, made to keep the number largest (Farsight, 10-ft UI) and the picture uncropped. They should be tested in the first usability round.

07 · Recommended

Glass: the oven’s own screen, as a calm card that opens into a cooking panel

Glass keeps what June owners know from the oven’s door: the ring with the big number inside it, the glowing element tiles, and the dark glass. The revision adds what the research asks for: status in words, a current-versus-target bar while preheating, an uncropped camera, a theme that follows Home Assistant, sizes for every screen, and the safety rules.

Layer 1 · The card

  • Name and a state pill (colour + word + shape), then one status sentence in plain words
  • The ring with the big number inside: temperature while preheating, time left while a timer runs, “Ready” or “Done” as words
  • Camera window beside the ring: the full 4:3 frame with its frame age; tap for live view. When idle: “Camera off · Turn on”
  • A current-versus-target bar under the camera while preheating or ready
  • One action: Stop (never confirmed) while heating
  • Idle: the oven’s home screen. The clock and date centered at the top, with the oven’s name on one side and a small camera window on the other, then square tiles across the full width in pages swiped left and right with page dots, as on June’s oven, with equal 8 px gaps both ways. The last tile is Settings, the same square with a neutral gear. Tapping a dot or an arrow turns the page without swiping. Mode tiles open the Cook page with that mode chosen; they never heat on one tap
  • While a timer runs: +1 · +5 · +10 min beside Stop, with Undo after each tap
  • While cooking or done: swipe to page 2, the temperature graph (oven, food, door openings); back to page 1 after 30 s
  • While cooking, tap the card to open the oven panel; Settings is also the gear in its header

Layer 2 · The panel

  • Now: the cook at full size, the stage track, temperature graph, adjust temperature and timer
  • Cook: mode tiles with element glyphs, temperature stepper and preset chips, timer and probe, then a review sheet
  • Camera: still first, live on request, stops itself after 5 minutes
  • History: past cooks, who started each one and from where, and the graph
  • Settings (gear in the header): alerts, camera, oven information, remote-start status. See Settings.
  • More than one oven? See Several ovens.

Opens as HA’s adaptive dialog: a bottom sheet on phones, a dialog on desktop. Deep-linkable (#oven-now, #oven-camera) from notifications.

Type. The Glass card uses Barlow for words and Barlow Condensed for numbers: open-licensed (SIL OFL) stand-ins for the DIN Next family June used, which is commercially licensed. It is set in regular weight rather than June’s light, because the research on 10-ft screens and Farsight shows thin strokes fade at a distance. The rest of the study keeps July’s Fraunces and Public Sans.

Appearance: Match Home Assistant, Light or Dark

Match Home Assistant is the default. It follows each viewer’s own light or dark theme (HA passes it to cards through its theme variables), so a shared wall tablet and someone’s phone can each look right. Dark is the oven’s own glass, and suits an always-on wall panel at night. Light is its daytime twin for bright kitchens. All three use July’s tokens and pass WCAG AA; status colours shift together so each state keeps its meaning in every appearance. Appearance is chosen in the card editor (see Settings).

Three sizes, chosen by the box the card is given

In Home Assistant the user resizes a card by dragging it to a number of columns and rows, so the card never chooses its own size. Glass reads the box it is given with CSS size container queries (container-type: size) and picks a layout that fits it; a display_mode option overrides it. Glance is for wall tablets read from 2–3 m, standard for phones and desktops, compact for a one-row strip. The camera is present at every size, and always secondary to the number. These follow the Glass appearance switch above.

The temperature graph and quick add time

Two things from the panel come onto the Glass card. The temperature graph shows the oven and the food over the whole cook, with every door opening marked. On the standard card it is a second page you swipe to, like the oven’s own screens. Page 1 stays the ring and camera, and the status line, Stop and quick add stay in place on both pages. On a wall tablet (glance) there is room to show it under the camera. Quick add (+1, +5, +10 min) sits beside Stop while a timer runs.

How the graph is drawn

  • The oven line is ember and the food line is ink, thicker, with a dashed line at the food’s target. Ember already means cooking; amber would mean preheating, so the panel’s amber food line is now ink too.
  • Each line is labelled in words at its end, with no legend to decode (Tufte’s direct labelling).
  • A door opening is a shaded band with “Door” above it. It comes from the oven’s door switch, never from guessing at a temperature dip. If the door sensor is missing, no band is drawn.
  • After 30 s the card goes back to page 1, so a wall tablet never rests on the graph.

How quick add behaves

  • It shows only while a timer is running. When preheating, ready or done there is nothing to extend.
  • It is one group reading “+1 · +5 · +10 min”, with “min” said once to save width. Screen readers hear “Add 1 minute”.
  • Each tap uses the oven’s own timer command (11006). The card then shows the new end time with Undo for a few seconds, so a wrong tap costs nothing.
  • Stop keeps its place, its size and its one tap. Quick add is outlined, never filled, so it can’t be mistaken for the main action.

08 · Settings

Where each setting lives

The first design had no settings at all. Going back to the research, settings split three ways by who changes them and how often. Each has an established home, so the card doesn’t invent a new one.

What the sources say

  • June kept settings apart from cooking, but on the same screen. The app had its own Settings screen (Info, Notifications, Disconnect, Help). On the oven, the home screen is pages of tiles you swipe left and right, with page dots; Settings comes right after the last cook mode (after Keep Warm), drawn as a grey gear so it never reads as a cooking tile.
  • Remote start is controlled at the oven. June: “Visit Settings › App Permissions on the oven.” GE, Samsung and Miele also arm remote start at the appliance. The card shows the status and never offers a toggle.
  • Card options belong in the card editor. HA asks custom cards to ship visual editors (Dashboard chapter 2), and Mushroom’s “visual editor for every option” is a big part of why it is loved.
  • Connection settings belong to the integration. The oven address, certificate and defaults already live in HA’s Configure screen for the integration.
  • Keep them out of the way of cooking. HA: one clear primary action, don’t clutter. NN/g: rarely used options go in a secondary view, and at the end of the mode list is exactly that. HA’s own more-info dialog also puts a gear in its header.
  • No hidden gestures. Many HA cards open settings with a long-press; older-adult research shows long-press is undiscoverable and fires by accident. Settings is a labelled button.

Decision

Three homes, one per audience. The panel’s Settings page gathers what people who cook need, and links out to the other two, so nobody has to know where a setting “really” lives. It has two doors: the Settings tile on the last page of the idle card’s mode tiles, exactly where June’s oven puts it, and the gear in the panel header, which is the way in while something is cooking and the tiles are hidden.

09 · Several ovens

For households with more than one June

The integration already supports several ovens: each is added once and becomes its own Home Assistant device with its own entities. The card needs to show them without ever hiding one that is hot.

What the research says

  • Never hide a heating oven. Visibility of system status (Nielsen) and the study’s safety rules apply to every oven, not just the one on screen. ISA-101 keeps abnormal or active states visible in every view, and HA’s 2026.9 Security dashboard surfaces “Active alerts” wherever you are.
  • One card, one clear primary action (HA dashboard principles). A card that controls two ovens must make it unmistakable which one a button acts on.
  • Status is colour, shape and word (IBM Carbon). Each oven’s chip carries all three, so a hot oven stands out even when it is not selected.
  • Start names its target. The review sheet says “Start preheating the Garage oven”, and every alert names the oven, so nobody acts on the wrong one.
  • Plain names. Use the name the household gave the oven or its area (“Kitchen”, “Garage”), per HA’s plain-language direction.

Decision

  • Separate cards (default). One Glass card per oven, side by side in a section. This is how Home Assistant treats devices, and both ovens are always visible.
  • Switcher in one card (opt-in, for phones and small wall tablets). A row of oven chips replaces the name; each chip shows that oven’s live state. An oven that is off shrinks to a small, unfilled pill (“○ Kitchen · Off”), so ovens that are heating take the room. The selected oven is underlined, as in Home Assistant’s tabs. The card grows to 12 × 7. The panel title becomes the same switcher.
  • List (opt-in, for desktops). One compact row per oven, each with its own Stop.
  • Settings are per oven: the Settings tile and panel gear open the selected oven’s settings.

Starting a cook

Two deliberate steps, both labelled with their outcome. There is no preselected default, and no long-press or swipe anywhere. The review sheet restates everything and expires after 5 minutes, following UL 1026’s remote-programming window. Every refusal from the oven becomes a plain instruction.

Stopping

Stop is visible in every active state, at least 2 grid rows tall on wall tablets, and labelled “Stop cooking”. It is never confirmed and never hidden in a menu. The card shows “Stopping…” until the oven confirms with 10017 cancelled, then “Oven off”. There is deliberately no Undo: undoing a stop would be a one-tap remote start. Instead the card offers “Start again…”, which goes through the review step.

Adjusting a running cook

Temperature and timer changes are not confirmed. They apply after a 1-second pause in tapping, matching HA’s own thermostat debounce, and show “Setting 425°F…” until the oven acknowledges. Changing mode mid-cook needs the review step, because the oven restarts the cook.

Alerts to pair with the card

Shipped as blueprints, since no oven blueprints exist yet. Each cook gets one notification tag, so alerts replace each other instead of piling up.

12 · Design rules

Twenty-one rules, each with a test

  1. The hero number is the largest thing on the card. Standard mode 56 px or more in a 500 px section (48 px floor in a 358 px phone section), glance mode 120 px or more, Fraunces with tabular figures. Target is half the size or less.Test: 4 of 5 people read the temperature within ±5° in a 3-second glance, at 2 m in glance mode.
  2. Every state has a sentence, a pill word and a shape. Colour is never the only signal.Test: every state is identifiable in a greyscale screenshot and under deuteranopia simulation.
  3. Current versus target is a bar, never only a gauge. A dot marks the current value because the scale doesn’t start at zero, and a tick marks the target.Test: faster and more accurate “how close?” answers than a 270° ring in A/B.
  4. Stop is one tap, always visible, never confirmed. At least 48 px, 2 grid rows in glance mode, labelled “Stop cooking”.Test: 5 of 5 people stop the oven within 2 s of being asked.
  5. Starting heat takes two outcome-labelled steps. Mode and temperature, then a review sheet that restates everything and expires after 5 minutes.Test: no single tap anywhere on the dashboard can start heating.
  6. No drag-only control, no long-press, no swipe-only path. Steppers and chips everywhere; any dial also has buttons. The idle tile pages swipe like the oven, but every page is also one tap away on its dot or arrow. Actions fire on release.Test: full operation by keyboard, and by single taps only.
  7. Primary targets are 14 mm or larger. About 72–84 px on phones; 48 px is the floor for anything tappable.Test: automated bounding-box audit.
  8. Stale data looks stale. After 30 s without an update, show the age; after 2 min, dim the number to 50% and say “Oven not responding”.Test: unplug the oven; the card changes within 30 s.
  9. Commands show sending, confirmed or refused. Pressed state within 100 ms, “Sending…” until the ack, a plain-language result after, and an error with a retry at 10 s.Test: with 3 s and 15 s injected latency, nobody double-taps Start.
  10. Preheat progress never goes backwards. Opening the door holds the bar and adds a “Door opened” note. “Ready” appears only when the sensor reaches target.Test: logged progress is monotonic; no false Ready in 20 cooks.
  11. Estimates are honest. Show “Estimating…” until there’s evidence. Round to 1 min under 10 min, 5 min under an hour, and hide beyond 4 hours. Count down in m:ss only for the last 5 minutes, and freeze on stale data.Test: displayed ETA error stays inside its rounding band in 80% of logged cooks.
  12. Colour only for active or abnormal states. Off is neutral, ember appears only while heating, green for ready or done, red only for faults.Test: an off oven renders with no accent colour.
  13. Contrast holds in both themes. Text 4.5:1, key numbers 7:1, bars, ticks and focus rings 3:1.Test: automated contrast check in HA default light and dark themes.
  14. Screen readers hear milestones, not ticks. The countdown uses role="timer"; one polite status region announces preheated, 5 min left, done and probe reached; faults use role="alert".Test: 5 or fewer announcements in a 10-minute NVDA/VoiceOver cook.
  15. The camera is always on the card, and never auto-plays. Every size shows a camera still (or an honest “Camera off · Turn on” placeholder), refreshed at most every 2 s. Live view runs only when expanded, stops after 5 min, and every frame is labelled with its age.Test: the camera is visible at every density; the collapsed card uses no video bytes.
  16. Calm at rest. No animation while off; a slow warmth only while heating, removed under reduced motion; nothing flashes.Test: with reduced motion on, no element moves.
  17. Always-on panels dim when idle. Glance mode has a dark, low-luminance idle state with a small pixel shift every few minutes.Test: idle glance-mode luminance under 20% of active.
  18. One alert per event, each tied to an action. One tag per cook, a Live Activity for the countdown, no per-degree pushes.Test: 4 or fewer notifications in a normal cook with a probe.
  19. Feels native. Root is <ha-card>, sizes come from getGridOptions in multiples of 3 (Glass: 12 × 6 standard, 12 × 1 compact, 24 × 8 glance), content always fits the fixed box at any section width from 320 to 500 px, colours come from HA variables with July fallbacks, labels come from hass.formatEntityState, and the config has a visual editor.Test: card-mod works, and theme switches restyle it without a reload.
  20. Every action names its oven when there is more than one. Start, Stop, the review sheet and every alert say which oven; an oven that is heating is never hidden behind a switcher.Test: with two ovens, 5 of 5 people stop the right oven first time.
  21. Plain words. No June internals (“cook_bound”), no bare icons, active voice, and buttons that say what happens.Test: a cognitive walkthrough of start, stop, extend timer and “is it done?” finds no unclear labels.

13 · Validation plan

Prove it with the people who will use it

Week 1Heuristic evaluation (3–5 reviewers, Nielsen’s 10) and a cognitive walkthrough of the four core tasks, on clickable mockups.
Weeks 2–4Three RITE rounds × 5 people, at least half aged 65+. Round 1 puts Glass beside Status line. Tasks at arm’s length and at 2 m; one round with wet fingers.
Week 5Glance test: a 3-second exposure, then “What is the oven doing? Is it safe to walk away?”
BetaHACS pre-release to Project July testers, with cook logs to check estimate accuracy and false Ready rates.

Ship criteria: 100% Stop success, SUS 80 or above (top-10% band; the average is 68), glance accuracy of 90% or better, and zero unintended starts across all sessions.

14 · Open questions

What we still need to learn

  • Remote-start permission. Does june-local honour the oven’s own “App Permissions” setting (session-start-disabled)? If not, should the card add its own arm-at-the-oven step, like GE and Miele?
  • Which modes accept remote start? The stock app offered Bake and Roast only; only bake is proven in captures. Default to Bake and Roast until others are verified on a real oven.
  • Door state. The oven detects its door: it cuts the heating elements while the door is open and refuses to start. So the card treats the door as real data: bands on the graph and “Door open · heating paused” in the status line. The open question is only where june-local reads it: from the oven’s telemetry, or from the oven software directly. None of the three captured cooks carries it.
  • Probe frames. No capture includes a probe. The doneness UI needs real sensor_data.probe frames before it ships.
  • Estimates. eta.status and cook_time_remaining never appeared in captures. If the oven doesn’t send them locally, the card must compute its own estimate or show time elapsed instead.
  • Unattended cooking. Should the card, or a blueprint, turn the oven off automatically when it has been heating long after a timer ended? June planned this in 2019 and it may never have shipped.
  • Swiping for Settings. The oven pages its tiles left and right and puts Settings after the last cook mode, and so does Glass. The older-adult research warns that swipes are hidden and hard, so every page is also one tap away on its dot or the arrows (WCAG 2.5.7). Those dots are 24 px targets, the WCAG minimum but below this study’s 48 px rule. Test whether owners find Settings on the last page, and whether the dots are big enough.
  • Mode tiles on a wall tablet. Glass shows the oven’s mode tiles when idle. They only open the review step, but test whether owners read them as “start” buttons, especially on always-on panels.
  • Two ovens, one card. Test whether people act on the wrong oven with the switcher, and whether the chip of a hot, unselected oven draws enough attention without flashing.
  • Camera when idle. camerad only runs while cooking or when switched on. Should an idle card show the last frame from the previous cook (labelled with its age), or the “Camera off” placeholder? Does turning it on remotely need its own indicator on the oven?
  • Food detection. The oven still detects food locally (it refuses to preheat with food inside). Could it also power a “food is in” cue on the card?
